The Signal Was There. It Was Eight Seconds Late.

A week ago I wrote that my slip detector could not tell a robot stuck with its wheels spinning from a robot driving normally. The idea that came out of that failure was to stop estimating slip continuously and classify the ground instead: decide which surface you are on, then pick a speed for it. Slow on the slippery patch, nominal on grip. Two numbers instead of a probability.

This post is about measuring whether that was possible at all. It was not, and the reason surprised me. The information was in the data. It was just slow, and a policy does not get to wait.

Terrain is a state, slip is an event

The first thing that had to change was the label. The old one, is_slipping, answers "am I losing traction right now", which is an event that lasts a moment. "Which surface am I on" is a state that holds for four metres. One label cannot serve both questions, and I had been using the event label to study the state.

So the experiment now records ground-truth position next to every sample, and the label comes from that position against the patch boundaries, which the script reads out of the world file rather than me typing them in. While setting that up I noticed the worlds were already what I needed. Each one is grippy ground with a four-metre low-friction patch from 2.5 m to 6.5 m, then grip again, so every run already crosses both surfaces. I did not need a new world. I needed to read the one I had.

On the two slippery worlds the classifier scored below the majority baseline. Answering "slippery" for every sample without looking at a single sensor would have beaten the model by 26 and 30 points. The bottom two rows have a majority of 1.000, so any gain there is structurally at most zero and they carry no information about the failure. The first two rows are the result.

The ceiling test

At this point there are three different diagnoses and they lead to three different months of work: the model family is wrong, the training set is too small, or the information is not in the sensors. A cheap test separates them. Train the model on data that includes the test terrain and read the score. That is the ceiling, the best this feature set could ever reach if transfer were free.

I ran it at four feature window lengths, against a majority baseline of 0.645.

Two things read at once. There is a state signal in the data, and it keeps growing with the window, from 8 points above baseline to 22. And it does not transfer at any window length: every mean transfer gain is negative, and widening the window does not rescue it. Whatever part of the signal is real is a slow part.

Slow is fatal when the decision has a deadline

In an offline classifier a slow signal is fine. In a policy it is the whole problem, because the robot has to act while it is still on the patch. The patch is four metres. At the commanded 0.4 m/s a crossing costs about ten seconds. Here is the five-second window model on unseen terrain, after smoothing and hysteresis, which is what a policy would actually consume.

Eight and a half seconds to say the word, in a crossing that costs ten. On the 0.05 world it takes 13.2 s, longer than the crossing itself, and coverage drops to 22 percent.

The 51 percent is worse than it looks, in two ways. It averages two command profiles, 30 percent on the continuous run and 71 percent on the stop-and-go one. And the run gets covered at all partly because the robot is stuck there, so it occupies the patch far longer than a clean crossing would take. The detector is given more time in exact proportion to how badly the robot is already failing. That is backwards from what a controller needs.

It is not an artefact of the smoothing either. With raw features and no window at all the delay is 8.0 s, essentially the same. And buying coverage is not free: on the 0.20 world the same settings fire 28 percent of the time off the patch, so the robot would crawl for no reason a quarter of the way through a perfectly grippy world.

Why the fast part is missing

The reason sits in the feature definitions and I wrote it up when I found it. The features are derivatives, mostly the residual between wheel acceleration and body acceleration. A robot stuck at constant wheel speed has zero acceleration everywhere. A robot cruising at constant speed also has zero acceleration everywhere. Wheel speed reads 0.4000 in the first case and 0.3999 in the second, the largest effect size across thirteen features is 0.56, and the best single feature separates the two states at 0.552 balanced accuracy where a coin gets 0.500.

Put next to the ceiling curve, that gives the shape of the whole problem. The fast signal is the transition, the instant traction changes, and it is genuinely observable, which is why slip detection appeared to work early on. The state is only visible in slow statistics gathered over five and ten second windows. The policy needs the state, and it needs it in the first second.

What the rig fault did to these numbers

Two days after this measurement I found that the robot had been driving nose down at 13.3 degrees for the entire study, dragging its front edge, and that the dragging was manufacturing the slip I thought I was studying. Everything above was measured on that rig, and the section it lives in is marked invalid in my results file. I am not going to quietly reuse the numbers, so here is what survives and what does not.

The structural argument does not depend on the apparatus. Two constant-velocity conditions produce the same derivative. That is arithmetic, and a level robot does not change it.

The numbers do depend on it. The 0.552, the 8.3 s, the ceiling curve: all of it was measured on a tilted robot and none of it can be quoted for a level one until it is run again.

One neighbouring result has already been re-measured. On the corrected rig, on a two-degree ramp at 0.02 friction, the EKF removes 0.009 m of 6.852 m of odometry error, about a tenth of a percent. Before the fix the same comparison was 13 mm out of 4.11 m, three tenths of a percent. Different apparatus, same answer: fusing wheel odometry with an IMU does not repair slip error, because neither channel sees slip.

The observability exam itself is queued and not yet run, and I expect the ramp to give a different answer, which is the reason to run it. A robot stuck on a slope has a gravity component in the accelerometer and a wheel-versus-body mismatch that move together. That coupling is exactly what flat ground did not offer.

The three tests I run before blaming the model

Ceiling. Let the model see the test terrain and read the score. If the ceiling sits at the majority baseline, the information is not in the sensors and no architecture will put it there.

Transfer. If the ceiling is high and held-out performance is negative, it is a transfer problem, and more terrains or better invariances are worth the month.

Decision time. This is the one I did not have, and it is the one that decided this phase. Accuracy is scored over a whole run; a controller consumes the answer at one instant. Ask when the answer arrives relative to the window in which it is still worth having. A classifier that is right at second nine of a ten second crossing is not slightly worse than one that is right at second one. It is useless in a different way, and no amount of accuracy repairs it.

Limits

Simulation only, one differential-drive robot, one nominal speed of 0.4 m/s, and friction values chosen to make the effect measurable rather than realistic. Two command profiles per world. The observability claim is about this sensor set, wheel encoders and an IMU, and says nothing about a robot carrying anything else. And the rig caveat above applies to every number in this post.
