I Put a Paper on arXiv. The Number Travelled and the Error Bar Did Not.

On 2 September I posted the accepted version of a SoutheastCon paper to arXiv. Within a few days it had appeared on sites I have never submitted anything to: an aggregator page on Deep Learning Monitor, a bot account that reposts new cs.RO listings, an automated daily AI briefing.

Not one of them said anything false. They copied the abstract, which is what abstracts are for. That is the whole point of this post, and since the paper is mine I can be blunt about what survived the trip and what did not.

What travelled: 98.6 percent evidence traceability, 99.0 percent temporal continuity, 98.1 percent decision reconstructability. The word simulated travelled too, which is worth noting, because I had put it in the abstract.

What did not travel: a standard deviation of 7.0, one random seed, a fault model I invented, and three baselines that were never implemented.

First, what the thing is

TRACE stands for Transparent Reasoning Architecture for Credible Execution. It is a wrapper around a robot's decision loop that records, at every cycle, which sensor readings supported a belief, which beliefs supported an action, and which alternative actions were rejected and why. Four layers: semantic perception, belief reasoning, action synthesis, execution verification. The claim is not that it makes better decisions. It is that after an incident, an investigator can reconstruct the decision instead of guessing at it.

That is the claim I still stand behind. What follows is about the number attached to it.

What 98.6 percent actually measures

The evaluation is a Python simulation of a 50 by 30 metre warehouse. The robot decides at 10 Hz. Five scenarios, 100 decision cycles each, 500 in total, one fixed random seed.

The simulation does not run clean. I injected failures, at rates I chose:

Injected failureRate
LiDAR unavailable2%
Camera unavailable12%
Evidence link corruption3%
Causal edge write failure2%
Rationale truncation2%
Counterfactual generation timeout2.5%

So the traceability figure is a measurement of how the architecture holds up under a fault model that I wrote, at rates that I picked. Move camera dropout from 12 percent to 30 and the number moves with it. Nothing in the study tells you which rate is right, because nothing in the study came off a robot.

Then there is the spread. The headline is 98.6 ± 7.0. On a metric bounded at 100, a standard deviation of 7 means at least one scenario did noticeably worse than the average. Which one, and why, is the most interesting question in the table, and it is the first thing a card-sized summary drops.

The baselines were never run

The comparison table lists attention at 20 to 25, SHAP at 30 to 35, LIME at 25 to 30. The paper states plainly what those are: baseline comparison is based on the architectural capabilities of each method rather than direct implementation.

That sentence is in the paper. It is in none of the reposts, and it is not in the abstract either. On an aggregator card, 98.6 sitting next to 25 to 30 reads as a head-to-head result. It is not one. It is an argument about what each architecture can do in principle.

I would defend the argument. Post-hoc attribution explains a single prediction. An audit trail documents a sequence of decisions and the alternatives that were rejected. Those are different objects, and no amount of tuning turns the first into the second. But I have an argument and I ran the comparison are not the same claim, and putting both in the same table with the same number formatting makes them look identical.

What does survive

I am not withdrawing anything. Three things in that paper measure what they say they measure.

The overhead is real: 0.12 ms per decision cycle, roughly 1 percent of the budget at 10 Hz. That is a measurement of code, and code is exactly what it claims to be about.

The architectural distinction above holds on its own, independent of any number.

And the three metrics are defined so that someone else can compute them on their own system. Evidence traceability, temporal continuity, decision reconstructability are arithmetic over an audit log, not a score I award myself. A reader who disagrees with my fault model can keep the metrics and throw out my rates.

What it would take to earn the headline

Four things, in the order I would do them.

  1. More than one seed. One seed with a fixed scenario set is a reproducibility guarantee, not a sample.
  2. A fault model taken from logged hardware rates instead of chosen by me. Camera dropout on a real warehouse robot is a measurable quantity. I measured nothing; I assumed 12 percent.
  3. The baselines implemented and run over the same 500 cycles, so the table compares measurements to measurements.
  4. A robot.

Until those exist, the honest one-line summary is under an injected fault model, the architecture kept the trail intact. It is not auditability solved.

I have been here before

Earlier this year I built a wheel-slip detector that reached 0.869 accuracy and cut position error by 72 percent. What beat it was driving slower with no detector at all. The lesson was not that the detector was bad. It was that a component scoring well on its own metric is not evidence that it helps the system, and the only way to find out is to run the dumbest alternative you can think of and see whether it wins.

TRACE has not had that test. The dumbest alternative to a four-layer audit architecture is logging everything to a file and sorting it out afterwards. I do not know what that scores on the three metrics, because I did not run it. That is the experiment I owe this line of work, and it is more useful than another point of traceability.

Where the burden sits

I cannot ask aggregators to carry error bars. They are copying an abstract, automatically, at volume, and they are doing it correctly.

The burden is on the author, and the mechanism is visible in what happened here. The word simulated survived to every repost because I had put it in the abstract. Under an injected fault model and baselines not implemented did not survive, because I had left them in the body where an automated copy would never reach them.

Both sentences go in the next abstract. It costs about fifteen words, and it is the difference between a number a reader can check and a number that merely gets repeated.

The paper: arXiv:2609.02861 (free full text) and IEEE Xplore. The slip-detector story is in the original post and the correction that followed it.
