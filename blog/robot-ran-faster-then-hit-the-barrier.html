The Robot Ran 100 Metres Faster Than Bolt. Then It Hit the Barrier.

The second World Humanoid Robot Games closed in Beijing yesterday. Five days at the National Speed Skating Oval, 666 teams, 2,056 robots from 16 countries, 51 events split between athletics and scenario tasks like factory work, hotel service and emergency response.

The headline was the sprint. Tiangong Ultra, from the Beijing Innovation Center of Humanoid Robotics, ran the 100 metres in 9.39 seconds on the opening day, 8.86 in the semifinal, and 8.64 in the final. A year ago the winning time at the first edition of these games was 21.50 seconds.

I want to be clear about that number before I complicate it. Cutting a sprint time by 60 percent in twelve months is not a stunt. Bipedal running at that speed is a hard control problem, and whatever the teams did to their controllers, gearboxes and thermal limits, they did it fast. That part deserves respect and it is measurable.

The detail I keep thinking about

The Associated Press reported that several of the runners, the champion included, crashed into a padded barrier after crossing the finish line, that sparks came off some of them, and that staff ran over with fire extinguishers.

That is not a bug in the software. That is arithmetic, and it is the more interesting half of the result.

Stopping is the harder half

Acceleration you command. Deceleration you negotiate with the ground, and the ground is not a parameter you own.

Averaged over 8.64 seconds, 100 metres is 11.6 m/s. Braking from that speed has two separate limits and neither of them is motor torque.

The first is friction. Stopping distance is roughly the square of the speed over twice the deceleration, so at a full 1 g, which needs a friction coefficient near 1.0, you still need about 6.8 m of floor. Halve the friction and it becomes 13.6 m.

The second is geometry, and on a good surface it binds first. A walking machine can only decelerate as hard as it can plant a foot ahead of its own centre of mass. Take a humanoid with its centre of mass 0.8 m up and a braking step landing 0.15 m in front of it: that ratio caps the average deceleration near 0.19 g, and 11.6 m/s then needs something like 36 m to bleed off. Lunge harder, plant the foot 0.4 m ahead, and you get about 0.5 g and roughly 14 m. Those geometry numbers are mine, an order-of-magnitude estimate rather than a measurement of that robot, and the real figures belong to the team that built it. But the shape of the answer does not depend on the details. The stopping distance is tens of metres and the runoff behind a 100 m finish line is not.

So there is a padded barrier. The barrier is the honest part of the venue. It is the organisers acknowledging, in foam, that the machines can reach a speed they cannot undo.

Off the track, the other limit is the invisible one

On a speed-skating oval the friction is high, known and constant, so geometry is what binds and the engineering problem is well posed. In a warehouse it inverts. There is a wet patch by the loading door, dust on the polished concrete, a steel plate someone left out. Now the friction term is the one that moves, and the robot has to know its value before it commits to a braking step.

That is the question I have spent this year measuring on a much slower robot, and the answer is not encouraging. Wheel encoders and an IMU cannot tell you what the ground will give you. In my setup a robot stuck with its wheels spinning and a robot driving normally produce wheel speeds of 0.4000 and 0.3999 m/s. Across thirteen features the largest effect size between the two states is 0.56, and the best single feature separates them at 0.552 balanced accuracy where a coin gets 0.500. The features are derivatives, and both states are steady, so both states read zero. A terrain classifier did not rescue it either: the usable signal only appeared in five and ten second windows, and the decision arrived 8.3 seconds into a crossing that takes about ten.

The size of the effect is worth stating plainly. On a two degree ramp at 0.02 friction, the same speed command that produced a 0.34 percent odometry error on the grippy control arm produced 245 percent on the slippery one. The robot is not slightly wrong about where it is. It is wrong by more than the distance it travelled.

A sprinting humanoid needs that same quantity, faster, and needs it before the foot lands rather than after.

What happens to a number when it leaves the venue

8.64 seconds is faster than Usain Bolt's 9.58, and that comparison is the part most likely to survive into next week's slide decks. It should not. A time is defined together with the conditions that produced it, and here almost none of the conditions match: the surface, the start, the mass, the energy budget, and whether anything about the run has to generalise to a floor the machine has not seen.

The high jump result shows the same thing more sharply. A robot cleared 2.88 m, against 0.95 m at last year's games. The year-on-year delta is the real content. The comparison being made in headlines, against Javier Sotomayor's 2.45 m, is a category error, because a standing jump and a jump with a run-up are different events with different physics.

I am not scoring points here. I published a slip result of my own with 0.869 accuracy and a 72 percent error reduction, and I later had to mark the whole section invalid because my robot had been driving nose down at 13.3 degrees and its dragging chassis was manufacturing the effect I thought I was measuring. Nobody made me quote that number out of context. I did it to myself, in public. The lesson stuck precisely because it was expensive.

The measurement I would like to see next

One event would tell the field more than the whole sprint programme, and it is cheap.

Bring the robot to full speed, command a stop, and report the stopping distance and the heading error at rest. Run it twice: once on the competition floor, once on a surface the robot has never seen and has not been told about. Publish both, and publish the failures rather than only the completed runs.

The gap between those two arms is the number that predicts whether a humanoid can work near people. Jonathan Hurst of Oregon State University made the general version of this point to the AP: true progress will be measured in warehouses and factories, where humanoids work autonomously for hours and generate real economic value. A stopping test is one concrete way to start measuring it.

What I could not verify

The autonomy rules matter for reading any of these times, and I could not pin them down to my satisfaction. The reporting I can verify says the 400 m, the 1,500 m and the 4×100 m relay ran as fully autonomous categories with no remote control. Several secondary sites state the same for the 100 m, but I could not confirm it in a source I trust, so I am not claiming it either way. If the sprint was teleoperated in any part, the record means something different again, and that is exactly the kind of condition that should travel attached to the number.

Sources: the results and scale from the World Humanoid Robot Games record and from Al Jazeera; the finish-line crashes, the final time and the Hurst quote from the AP report as carried by OPB. My own numbers come from a ROS 2 study in a private repository; the measurement write-ups are here and here.
