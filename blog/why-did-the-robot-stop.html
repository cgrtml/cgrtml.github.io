A Warehouse Robot Stops and a Worker Walks Into It. Can Anyone Find Out Why?

Start with the scene, because everything else follows from it. A mobile robot is moving down an aisle. It stops without warning. A worker walking behind it does not, and there is a collision. Nobody is seriously hurt, but somebody now has to write a report explaining what happened.

The investigator has the logs. The robot recorded its sensor streams and its motor commands, and at 14:22:07 the command was stop. Between those two things sits a neural network that turned camera and LiDAR readings into that command through several million parameters. The log tells you what the robot did. It does not tell you what the robot believed, or what it would have done instead, or which reading changed its mind.

That gap is the subject of a paper I published at SoutheastCon 2026, and this post is the version without the equations.

Why this is not only an academic complaint

Service robot sales grew 30 percent in 2023 according to the International Federation of Robotics, and these machines are not in cages any more. They share aisles with warehouse staff, roads with pedestrians, and operating theatres with surgeons.

The auditability gap turns into three concrete problems as soon as deployment is real.

Incident investigation becomes speculative. Without a reconstructable record, the report is a plausible story rather than a finding, and the fix that follows is aimed at a guess.

Safety certification becomes difficult. Standards such as ISO 13482 require documented risk assessment, and a system that cannot say why it acted cannot supply the document.

Regulatory compliance fails outright. The EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to keep logs that enable tracing of the system's functioning. Most deployed robotic systems today cannot satisfy that sentence, and it is not a sentence you can satisfy retroactively.

Why explaining the model does not solve it

The obvious answer is to reach for explainable AI, and the obvious answer is aimed at the wrong object.

LIME approximates a complex model with a simple one near a single prediction. SHAP attributes a prediction to input features using game theory. Both are useful and both explain one output. A robot makes hundreds of interdependent decisions per second, and explaining each of them in isolation loses the chain that connects them, which is the only thing the investigator wanted.

Attention weights look like a way out, since the model appears to be telling you what it looked at. Jain and Wallace showed that attention weights often fail to reflect the actual causal relationships. For a safety-critical system, an explanation that is confidently wrong is worse than no explanation, because it ends the investigation early.

Runtime verification is closer. Systems like ModelPlex check that a cyber-physical system stays inside verified bounds, which is genuinely valuable, but a violation flag says that something went out of bounds and not why the controller chose to go there.

So the distinction I would put at the centre of all of this: post-hoc methods explain a model output, and an investigation needs a decision sequence. Those are different objects, and no amount of tuning turns the first into the second.

Building the record instead of recovering it

TRACE stands for Transparent Reasoning Architecture for Credible Execution. The idea is simple enough to state in one line: stop trying to extract explanations out of an opaque system afterwards, and make the decision pipeline emit the evidence as it runs.

It is a wrapper, not a replacement. The perception module can stay a convolutional network, the predictor can stay a transformer. What the architecture requires is that each stage expose its intermediate product in a documented form. Four layers, each producing one artifact.

Semantic perception. A conventional pipeline outputs a detection: pedestrian, 3.2 metres ahead, confidence 0.87. This layer outputs the detection together with its evidence set, which records each sensor that contributed, the weight of its contribution, and the spatial region it came from. The difference matters later, when the question is whether the robot saw a person or saw a reflection.

Belief reasoning. Above perception sits a probabilistic scene graph holding things the robot cannot observe directly, such as whether a pedestrian intends to cross or whether an aisle is blocked. Derived states enter the graph as child nodes with explicit edges to the observations that produced them. When the robot believes the aisle is blocked, the graph names the readings responsible, and the belief stops being an assertion and becomes a claim with parents.

Action synthesis. This is the layer I would defend hardest. The planner does not only record the command it issued. It records the alternatives it considered, the rationale for the selection, and the counterfactual conditions that would have produced a different choice. The structure is a Counterfactual Decision Tree, and it exists to answer the two questions an investigator actually asks: why did the robot stop, and what would have made it continue.

Execution verification. The last layer compares intended behaviour with what the machine did, timestamps the whole cycle into one audit record, and flags the deviation when the two disagree.

Three numbers an outside engineer can compute

A framework that grades itself is not worth much, so the metrics are defined as arithmetic over the logged artifacts. Someone who has never met me can compute all three from their own system's logs.

Evidence traceability is the fraction of the factors behind a decision that can be followed back to a specific sensor reading through a documented chain. There is also a partial version, which matters more than it sounds: when a chain is incomplete, it reports how deep the documentation goes. Depth 2 out of 3 tells the investigator that the immediate and secondary causes are recorded and the root observation is missing, which is a far more useful answer than a failure.

Temporal continuity is one minus the proportion of the analysis window with no recorded decision. It measures whether the trail has holes in it, which is the first thing that goes wrong under load.

Decision reconstructability is the strict one. It counts a cycle only when all three conditions hold together: the action taken is recoverable, the alternatives considered are recoverable, and the reason the selected action was preferred is recoverable. Two out of three scores zero, because two out of three does not let anyone reconstruct anything.

What it measured

The evaluation is a warehouse simulation, 50 by 30 metres, decisions at 10 Hz, five scenarios running 100 cycles each. The scenarios go from a clear aisle up to a forklift on a collision course, and the simulation does not run clean: sensor dropouts, corrupted evidence links, truncated rationales and counterfactual timeouts are all injected.

MetricTRACE
Evidence traceability98.6% ± 7.0
Temporal continuity99.0%
Decision reconstructability98.1%
Overhead per decision cycle0.12 ms

The overhead is the number I care about most, because it is the one that decides whether anybody will accept the architecture. At 10 Hz the budget per cycle is 100 ms, so the audit trail costs about 1 percent of it. Auditability at that price is not a trade-off anyone has to think hard about.

One honest note on the comparison table in the paper. The post-hoc baselines are placed there on the basis of what each architecture can do in principle, not on runs I performed. That is stated in the paper and I will restate it here, because the distinction I am drawing is architectural and it should be read as an argument rather than as a measured contest.

The part that decides whether anyone can run it

Papers in this area tend to stop at the metrics. The question that actually determines deployment is where you put all of this.

Full audit trails run 2 to 5 KB per decision cycle. At 10 Hz that is 70 to 175 MB per hour, or 1.7 to 4.2 GB per robot per day. Now take a fleet of a hundred thousand robots and the six-month retention that Article 19 of the EU AI Act asks for, and the raw requirement passes 30 petabytes.

That number kills the idea unless the storage is tiered, so the design tiers it. Hot storage keeps the past 24 hours in full detail, which covers the window where incidents are actually investigated. Warm storage keeps flagged decisions complete and everything else as summaries. Cold storage holds a compressed archive for the regulator.

Compression helps more here than it would elsewhere, because causal graphs repeat themselves. The same structures recur cycle after cycle, so dictionary encoding is projected to give 3 to 5 times reduction, and delta encoding between consecutive cycles a further 60 to 80 percent on routine operation where the belief state barely moves. Those two figures are projections from preliminary analysis rather than measurements, and I would like them measured.

What is not settled

All of it ran in simulation, with modelled sensor noise and modelled failures. Validation on physical hardware with real sensors is necessary before anyone draws a deployment conclusion, and the paper says so in as many words.

There is also an experiment I owe this work. Earlier this year I built a wheel-slip detector that scored well on its own metric and was beaten by simply driving slower with no detector at all. The lesson stuck: a component that looks good in isolation is not evidence that it helps, and the way to find out is to run the dumbest alternative you can think of. Here the dumbest alternative is logging everything to a file and sorting it out afterwards. I do not know what that scores on the three metrics, because I have not run it, and until I do, the honest claim is that the architecture keeps the trail intact rather than that it is the only thing that could.

What I would defend today is narrower and, I think, more durable than a percentage: an audit trail is a design requirement, not a reporting feature. If it is not in the architecture while the robot is deciding, it is not recoverable afterwards, and the investigation that needed it will be a story instead of a finding.
